Module 2: Traffic Safety, teacher guide

Why speed decides who walks away

Pupils calculate stopping distances and discover why a few km/h change what happens when a child steps out.

Age: written for about 10 to 14 years; adapt the language and the numbers Time: 2 sessions of 45 minutes, plus a short supervised outdoor measurement You need: stopwatches or phones, a tape measure or trundle wheel, chalk or cones, calculators

Learning objectives

By the end of this module pupils can:

  • explain the difference between reaction distance and braking distance
  • use the formulas to estimate a stopping distance
  • explain why braking distance grows faster than speed
  • name at least two design or behaviour measures that reduce risk near a school

Running the interactive activity

Answers. The highest speeds at which the car still stops short are 25 km/h in situation 1 (wet, 15 m), 25 km/h in situation 2 (distracted driver, 20 m) and 30 km/h in situation 3 (dry, alert driver, 12 m). The activity accepts only the exact limit, so pupils have to explore around it.

Worked example for the board. 30 km/h is 8.33 m/s. Reaction at 1 s: 8.3 m. Braking: 8.33² ÷ (2 × 0.7 × 9.81) = 5.1 m. Total 13.4 m.

Suggested prompts. “What happens to the red part when you go from 30 to 50?”, “Which would help more: a better driver or a slower car?”

Likely misconceptions

  • “The car stops as soon as the driver sees the child.” Reaction time comes first; the car keeps its speed during it.
  • “5 km/h more is nothing.” At 30 to 35 km/h the braking distance grows by about a third, and the impact speed when it fails grows fast.
  • “It is the child's fault for stepping out.” Move the discussion to design: what in the street makes a normal mistake dangerous?

Discussion questions

  1. Which measure would cut risk most outside our school: lower speed, a raised crossing, no parking near the crossing, or better lighting?
  2. Why do roads near schools often have a 30 km/h limit and not 40?
  3. What can pedestrians do, and what should drivers and planners do?

A note on handling the topic

This module talks about child injuries and deaths. Keep the tone factual, not frightening. Some pupils may have been affected by a road crash. Tell them in advance what the topic is, and let anyone step out of the discussion.

Differentiation

  • Support: work with one situation at a time, and use only the dry-road case first; let pairs run the activity before the formulas are introduced.
  • Stretch: ask pupils to derive the braking distance formula from v² = 2as, and to find how much the safe speed changes if the road is icy (friction about 0.1).

Checking understanding

The quiz has six questions with explanations. Look for pupils who can say why braking distance grows with the square of the speed, not only that it does. For the worksheet, check that the measured speeds are computed from time (72 ÷ seconds for 20 m) and that the stopping distance is calculated with the formula.

This guide is a starting point. Adjust the timing, group sizes and fieldwork to your class and to your school's own safety rules.
