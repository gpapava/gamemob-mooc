Reading the emissions of a journey

Pupils turn their own school run into grams of CO₂, find where the emissions come from and plan how to cut them.

Age: written for about 10 to 14 years; adapt the language and the numbers Time: 2 sessions of 45 minutes You need: a calculator for each pair, the survey sheet from the worksheet, a way to count hands

Learning objectives

By the end of this module pupils can:

  • calculate the emissions of a journey from distance, factor and number of trips
  • explain why occupancy changes the emissions per person
  • identify which trips cause most of a class's emissions
  • describe at least three school-level measures that cut emissions and traffic at the gate

Running the interactive activity

Baseline. Today the class emits about 2,398 kg of CO₂ a year and 15 cars stop at the gate. The target is a cut of at least 60% and no more than four cars.

One possible answer. Under 1 km: all 8 walk. 1 to 3 km: 1 walks, 6 cycle, 3 take the bus. Over 3 km: all 6 take the bus. Result: about 832 kg a year (a cut of 65%) and no cars. Pupils can also use car pools for the longest trips.

What to draw out. The pupils who live over 3 km are a quarter of the class and cause more than half of the emissions. Ask pupils what the long trips need that the short ones do not.

Honesty about the numbers. The emission factors are rounded teaching values. Say so, and point to the assumptions box in the activity.

Likely misconceptions

  • “Short trips cause most of the emissions.” They are the easiest to change, but the long trips carry the carbon.
  • “An electric car is zero emissions.” It removes exhaust emissions, but not congestion, road danger or the space needed.
  • “One person cannot make a difference.” Show the class total: individual choices add up and become visible at the gate.

Discussion questions

  1. Which pupils could easily change how they travel, and which would need help?
  2. Is it fair to ask pupils who live far away to change? What could the school or city offer them?
  3. Would you prefer the smallest carbon number or the fewest cars at the gate? Do they always agree?

A note on handling the topic

Travel habits depend on family circumstances. Do not ask pupils to put their names or addresses on the survey and do not compare individuals. Use bands (under 1 km, 1 to 3 km, over 3 km), not exact distances.

Differentiation

  • Support: give pupils the band totals already filled in, and focus on one band at a time.
  • Stretch: ask pupils to add a fourth distance band, or to estimate emissions for the whole school using the class as a sample, and to say how uncertain that is.

Checking understanding

The quiz has six questions with explanations. Look for pupils who can explain the formula and say which pupils matter most. For the worksheet, check the arithmetic and whether the proposed measures match the distance bands.

This guide is a starting point. Adjust the timing, group sizes and fieldwork to your class and to your school's own safety rules.
