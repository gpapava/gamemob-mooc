Module 4 · Active Mobility · Teacher guide

Why short trips belong to people

Pupils guess, then test, when the car beats the bike door to door, and look at their own route with a cyclist's and walker's eye.

Age: written for about 10 to 14 years; adapt the language and the numbers Time: 2 sessions of 45 minutes, plus a week for the journey log You need: stopwatches or phones, a printed local map or a map app, coloured pens

Learning objectives

By the end of this module pupils can:

  • define active mobility and name two benefits for children
  • explain what door-to-door time includes and why it matters
  • read a chart to find where one mode overtakes another
  • name the five requirements of a good cycling route and rate a real route against them

Running the interactive activity

Answers with the default settings (bus every 10 minutes, bike parking at the door): the car overtakes the bike at about 4.5 km in the rush hour and at about 2.2 km on free-flowing roads. If there is nowhere obvious to lock the bike, the rush-hour crossover falls to about 3.2 km.

What to look for. Most pupils guess too high, because they think of the speed of the car and forget the minutes spent parking and walking. Ask them which line moved when they changed an assumption.

Suggested prompts. “What would make the bike line lower?”, “What would make the bus competitive?”

Likely misconceptions

  • “A faster vehicle means a faster journey.” Count the minutes at both ends.
  • “Cycling is dangerous.” Often it is the road that is dangerous. Ask what would change if the lane were protected.
  • “Everyone can cycle.” Not every pupil has a bike, a safe route or the ability. Use words such as walk, scoot and wheel.

Discussion questions

  1. Which part of the door-to-door trip would you change first to get more people cycling?
  2. Who cannot use the active option on your way to school? What would it take?
  3. Is a 3 km trip in the rain the same as in sunshine? What would you want from the school or the city?

A note on handling the topic

Not every pupil has a bike or lives close enough to walk. Keep the language inclusive, and do not rank pupils by how they travel. The journey log can be done on paper or by describing a trip they took with a family member.

Differentiation

  • Support: use only walking and the bus in the worksheet, and provide the speeds so that pupils only compute times.
  • Stretch: ask pupils to add e-bikes (for example 20 km/h) to the model and find the new crossover, and to discuss what an e-bike changes for hills and long routes.

Checking understanding

The quiz has six questions with explanations. A strong answer in the worksheet connects at least two of the five requirements to a specific spot on the route and proposes a concrete fix.

This guide is a starting point. Adjust the timing, group sizes and fieldwork to your class and to your school's own safety rules.
