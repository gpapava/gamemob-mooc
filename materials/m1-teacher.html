Module 1: Sustainable Mobility Basics, teacher guide

How a street shares its space

Pupils learn that moving people is a question of space, then redesign a street and see the trade-offs.

Age: written for about 10 to 14 years; adapt the language and the numbers Time: 2 sessions of 45 minutes You need: a photo of the street outside your school, one device per pair, tape measure or trundle wheel (for the worksheet)

Learning objectives

By the end of this module pupils can:

  • name the three pillars of sustainable mobility and give an example of each
  • place five travel modes in the hierarchy and explain the order
  • compare how many people a car lane, a bus lane and a bike lane can carry
  • propose a street design and explain who gains and who gives something up

Running the interactive activity

Setup. Nothing to install. The activity runs in the browser and remembers where a pupil left off.

What to look for. Pairs that only add bus and bike lanes will run out of width. Ask what they are willing to remove. The strongest designs keep some parking or one car lane and explain why.

One possible answer (not the only one): wide pavement, bike lane, parking, car lane, bus lane, car lane, planter, pavement. It fits in 20 m, moves about 15,900 people an hour, and gives 40% of the width to cars and parking, against 80% at the start.

Suggested prompts. “Who lost space in your design?”, “Why did you put the parking where you did?”, “What does the number not tell you?”

Likely misconceptions

  • “A painted bike lane next to traffic is protected.” It is not. The activity flags it; ask pupils to explain why.
  • “The numbers are what the street will carry.” They are what it could carry if demand existed. A new bike lane needs people to want to cycle.
  • “Sustainable mobility means getting rid of cars.” It means giving priority to the modes that use space and cause harm least, and keeping cars for the trips that need them.

Discussion questions

  1. If you could change only one strip on the street outside our school, which would it be and why?
  2. Who might object to your design? What would you say to them?
  3. Why might adding another car lane make congestion worse in the long run?

A note on handling the topic

Some pupils are driven to school every day for good reasons: distance, disability, parents' work hours. Keep the discussion about the street, not about families' choices.

Differentiation

  • Support: give pairs a partly built street (for example already with a bus lane) and ask them to finish it; use the photo to name the strips first.
  • Stretch: ask for a design under a stricter rule, such as a one-way street with only 14 m of width, and for a written justification of each choice.

Checking understanding

The module quiz has six questions with explanations. A pupil who has met the objectives should answer the hierarchy, induced demand and bus-lane questions without help. In the activity, success means all five goals are ticked and the pupil can explain one trade-off in their own words.

This guide is a starting point. Adjust the timing, group sizes and fieldwork to your class and to your school's own safety rules.
