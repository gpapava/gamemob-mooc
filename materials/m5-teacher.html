Module 5 · Innovation · Teacher guide

Technology as a tool, not the goal

Pupils compare three ways of controlling a crossing, discuss what technology can and cannot do, and plan a small pilot of their own.

Age: written for about 10 to 14 years; adapt the language and the numbers Time: 2 sessions of 45 minutes You need: coloured cards (or paper) for the role-play, a stopwatch, one device per pair

Learning objectives

By the end of this module pupils can:

  • describe how a fixed timer, a push button and a sensor differ
  • read results from a simulation and explain the trade-off between pedestrian wait and car delay
  • raise at least three non-technical questions about any smart system, such as privacy, failure and access
  • plan a small pilot with a clear problem, a simple intervention and a way to measure success

Running the interactive activity

The brief (school peak): average pedestrian wait 6 s or less, average car delay 8 s or less, nobody waits more than 30 s, nobody crosses on red.

What the results show. The sensor controller meets the brief across a wide range of settings (it worked in about 3 of every 10 combinations we tried), for example 16 s minimum car green with an 8 s maximum walk: pedestrians wait about 4.5 s and cars about 7.7 s. The fixed timer and the push button only meet it in a very narrow band, for example a fixed 12 s car green with a 6 s walk.

A good question for pupils. That fixed setting meets the brief, but a 6 s walk phase is very short for a slow walker. The brief did not check that. What else should a brief include? (Accessibility, crossing time, safety margins.)

Honesty about the model. The traffic is simulated with made-up arrival rates. It shows a pattern, not a measurement of any real crossing.

Likely misconceptions

  • “Smart means better.” A technology is better only if it solves the problem at an acceptable cost and risk.
  • “Sensors watch people, so they always break privacy.” Not necessarily: counting without identifying is possible, but it is a design choice.
  • “A short wait is only a comfort.” Long waits make people cross on red, so waiting time is also a safety issue.

Discussion questions

  1. Who might be left out by a solution that needs a phone or a camera?
  2. What should the crossing do if the sensor stops working?
  3. Is there a cheaper, simpler fix for the same problem? Try one for your school.

A note on handling the topic

Be careful with any activity that involves filming or tracking pupils. The role-play in the worksheet uses cards and a stopwatch, not cameras. If pupils propose camera-based ideas, use it as a chance to discuss consent and data protection.

Differentiation

  • Support: run the simulation with the sensor controller only and ask pupils to move one slider at a time and write down what changed.
  • Stretch: ask pupils to design a fourth controller that gives more time automatically when it detects a slow walker, and to describe what data it needs and the risks.

Checking understanding

The quiz has six questions with explanations. In the pilot canvas, look for a problem that is specific, a measurement that can actually be taken (for example the number of cars at the gate, or seconds waited) and at least one risk named.

This guide is a starting point. Adjust the timing, group sizes and fieldwork to your class and to your school's own safety rules.
