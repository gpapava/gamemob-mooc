Reading the emissions of a journey

The activity turned a school run into grams of CO₂. This page explains the arithmetic and what it leaves out.

Transport and the climate

Transport has been one of the slowest parts of the European economy to cut its emissions. It produces roughly a quarter of the EU's greenhouse gas emissions, and road transport is by far the largest share of that, with passenger cars the biggest single part. Because a school run is made every morning by many families, small choices add up in a way that is easy to measure.

The arithmetic

The activity uses one formula:

emissions = pupils × distance × factor × trips per day × school days

The factor is grams of CO₂ per passenger-kilometre, which is why occupancy matters. The same car carrying three pupils emits a third as much per pupil as when it carries one.

These are rounded teaching values. Published averages differ by fuel, vehicle age and occupancy, so a real study should use an agreed source.

A quarter of the class causes more than half the emissions. The short trips are the easy ones to switch, and the campaigns usually focus on them, but they hold little of the carbon. The long trips need different tools: a bus that actually serves the area, a car pool, a bike route with an e-bike option. This is the main lesson of the activity.

Not just carbon

Exhaust also contains nitrogen dioxide and fine particles, which harm lungs. Children breathe faster than adults and their lungs are still developing. Cars idling at the school gate create a short, concentrated peak at exactly the time children arrive. The WHO tightened its air quality guidelines in 2021 to 10 µg/m³ (annual mean) for nitrogen dioxide and 5 µg/m³ for PM2.5. Many European cities are above both. Cars also make noise and take up space, which is why the gate count in the activity matters even when the carbon number looks good.

What the number leaves out

  • Only the journey to school is counted. The parent's drive home is ignored, which flatters cars.
  • Only operating emissions. Making a car, a bus or a bike, and building roads, are not included.
  • An electric car cuts exhaust emissions, but it does not cut congestion, road danger or the space a car needs.

Measures that work at school level

  • Walking buses and bike trains: adults supervise groups on a fixed route.
  • Car-pool matching through the school, with a drop-off point a few minutes away from the gate.
  • Secure bike and scooter parking inside the school grounds.
  • A no-idling rule at the gate.

Before you move on

  1. Which group of pupils did you move first, and why? Would you do it differently after reading the table above?
  2. Is a 60% cut fair if it relies on pupils over 3 km from school changing how they travel? What would they need?
  3. Which has more value for a class: a smaller carbon figure or fewer cars at the gate? Could they pull in different directions?

Key terms

Passenger-kilometre
One person carried over one kilometre. Used to compare modes fairly.
Occupancy
The average number of people in a vehicle.
Operating emissions
Emissions released while a vehicle is in use, not counting its manufacture.
PM2.5
Particles smaller than 2.5 micrometres, small enough to enter the bloodstream through the lungs.
Walking bus
A group of children walking to school with adult supervisors along a set route.
