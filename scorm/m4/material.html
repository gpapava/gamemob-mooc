What counts as active mobility

Walking, cycling, scooting and wheeling: any travel powered mainly by the traveller. Electric assistance extends the range without changing the principle. It is not only exercise. For a child it is also independence, a daily routine and a way to know the neighbourhood.

Door to door, not speed limit to speed limit

People compare modes by speed, but what matters is the whole journey. Every mode has hidden minutes at both ends.

ModeTime that is not movingMoving speed
Walknone4.8 km/h
Bikeabout 1 min to lock up14 km/h
Bus5 min to the stop, 5 min waiting, 3 min walking on18 km/h in traffic
Car1 min to the car, 4 min parking, 3 min walking on (rush hour)22 km/h in traffic

The car's advantage is real but arrives late. On a short trip, 8 minutes of non-moving time has already used up what the extra speed would save. That is why the lines in the activity cross at a few kilometres, not at one or two hundred metres. The exact crossing depends on traffic, parking and bus frequency, which is why the activity lets you change them.

What makes people cycle

Dutch design guidance sums up what a good bike network needs in five words: cohesion (it connects origins and destinations), directness (no big detours), safety, comfort (smooth surface, few stops) and attractiveness. Notice that none of them is about the cyclist's fitness or attitude. Most people who say cycling is dangerous are describing the road, not themselves.

  • A lane painted next to fast traffic is used by confident adults. A protected lane is used by children and grandparents.
  • Parking matters as much as lanes. If the bike has to be carried upstairs or may be stolen, the trip does not happen.
  • Crossings and junctions are where most conflicts occur. A good route is only as good as its worst junction.

Micromobility

E-scooters, shared bikes and cargo bikes add options for trips that are too long to walk and too short to justify a car. They also bring new questions: where to park them, who they share space with, and how to keep speeds compatible with pavements and bike lanes. A rule of thumb in design guides is that very different speeds should not share the same narrow strip.

Safe routes to school

A practical way to start is to map where pupils actually walk and cycle, find the three or four worst crossings and fix those first. Combine this with supervised groups such as walking buses, and with a school street at drop-off times.

Designing for choice

Active travel is not for everyone on every day. Distance, weather, hills, disability, carrying things and fear of traffic all matter. The aim is not to force one mode but to make the active option a normal, safe choice for the many short trips that today default to the car.

Key terms

Door to door
The whole journey from the starting point to the final destination, including waiting, parking and walking at each end.
Micromobility
Small, lightweight vehicles for individual use: bikes, e-bikes, e-scooters and similar.
Modal shift
A change in how people travel, for example from car to bike.
Walking bus
A group of children walking to school with adult supervisors along a set route.
Network (cycling)
The connected set of routes. A single good lane that connects to nothing is rarely used.
