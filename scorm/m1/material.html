How a street shares its space

Moving people is mostly a question of space. This page explains the ideas behind the street you rebuilt in the activity.

Access, not movement

Nobody wants to travel for its own sake. People want to reach school, work, a doctor, a friend. Sustainable mobility is about providing that access while limiting the harm that travel causes: greenhouse gases, polluted air, noise, crashes, and the land that roads and car parks take up. It is usually described through three pillars: environmental (emissions, air, land), social (safety, health, access for people who do not drive) and economic (what the system costs to build, run and live with).

The hierarchy of modes

European planning guidance ranks travel modes by how much space and harm they impose, and asks cities to start from the top. It is not a ban on anything lower down. It is a rule for who gets the benefit of the doubt when the street is redesigned.

1. Walking smallest footprint, no emissions
2. Cycling and other active modes
3. Public transport
4. Shared and on-demand vehicles
5. Private car largest footprint per person

Space is the scarce resource

A street is a fixed width. Whatever you give to one use you take from another, so the useful question is how many people each strip can carry. Street design guides publish ranges for this. The figures in the activity are mid-range values from that kind of guide.

The ranges are wide for good reasons. A car lane carries more if cars have passengers and the junction ahead flows. A bus lane depends on how often buses come. A bike lane only carries 7,500 people an hour if there are 7,500 people wanting to cycle, which is not true on most streets today. Treat the numbers as an order of magnitude, not a forecast.

Why adding road does not cure congestion

If congestion were a shortage of road, widening roads would fix it. Studies of US cities found the opposite pattern: traffic grows almost in step with road capacity. New lanes make driving more attractive, so more trips are made by car, longer trips become possible, and some people switch from other modes. This is called induced demand. It is one reason cities now treat road space as something to reallocate, not only to extend.

What a school street can do with its metres

  • Wide pavement at the gate. Parents, buggies and children arrive in the same ten minutes. A 2 m pavement does not cope.
  • A protected bike lane. Painted lines beside moving traffic do little for a child. Parked cars, planters or a kerb between the lane and the traffic do.
  • Bus priority. A dedicated lane makes the bus predictable, which is what makes people choose it.
  • Trees and planters. Shade and cooling, and a physical edge that makes the street feel narrower, so drivers slow down.
  • Time-limited closure. Many cities close the street to through traffic at drop-off and pick-up times. This is often called a school street.

Before you move on

  1. In your design, who lost space? Would they have a reasonable alternative?
  2. Your street moves more people, but is it more pleasant to stand at the gate? What does the capacity figure not measure?
  3. Think of a street you know. Which of the five measures above would help it most?

Key terms

Modal share
The percentage of trips made by each mode, for example 40% walking, 10% cycling, 50% car.
Induced demand
Extra traffic that appears because road capacity was added.
Protected bike lane
A bike lane physically separated from moving traffic, by parked cars, a kerb, planters or a verge.
SUMP
Sustainable Urban Mobility Plan: the planning approach the European Commission recommends to cities.
Carriageway
The part of the street used by vehicles.
