Why speed decides who walks away

The activity asked how fast a car can go and still stop for a child. This page shows where the answer comes from.

Stopping takes two steps

A car does not begin to slow down when the driver sees the danger. First the driver notices, decides and moves a foot. That is the reaction time, typically about one second for an alert driver and two or more for a distracted one. During it the car keeps its full speed. Only then does the braking distance begin.

  • Reaction distance = speed × reaction time
  • Braking distance = speed² ÷ (2 × friction × 9.81)

Speed is in metres per second (divide km/h by 3.6). Friction is about 0.7 on dry asphalt and 0.4 on a wet road. Worn tyres, ice or gravel lower it further.

The square that surprises people

Going from 30 to 50 km/h is 67% faster, but the braking distance almost triples, because it grows with the square of speed. The reaction distance grows only in proportion. So a child who steps out 14 metres ahead is a near miss at 30 km/h (the car stops in 13.4 m on a dry road) and a nearly full-speed impact at 50 km/h: the car is still doing about 50 km/h when it reaches the child, because the whole 14 metres is used up before the brakes bite.

The Safe System idea

People make mistakes, and children make more of them: they are harder for drivers to see, and they are still learning to judge the speed and distance of cars. The Safe System approach accepts this and asks designers to make sure a mistake does not become a death. That means managing speed first, since it is the one variable that sets how much energy is in a crash, then designing crossings, visibility and separation around it. The 2020 Stockholm Declaration, agreed at a UN-backed ministerial conference, recommended 30 km/h limits where vulnerable road users and vehicles mix.

Habits that cost nothing

  • Cross at marked places, and wait until the car has actually slowed, not until it appears to.
  • Do not step out from behind a parked van or bus. The driver cannot see you until you are already in the road.
  • On a bike, use lights and reflective clothing at dusk, and choose the safer route even if it is longer.
  • Drivers: a two-second phone glance at 30 km/h covers about 17 metres without braking. That is situation 2 in the activity.

Key terms

Reaction time
The time between a hazard appearing and the driver starting to brake.
Braking distance
The distance the car travels while it is actually slowing down.
Friction coefficient
A number describing grip between tyre and road. Lower means longer stops.
Vulnerable road users
People without a protective shell: pedestrians, cyclists, riders of mopeds and scooters.
Safe System
A road-safety approach that designs the system around human error and the limits of the human body.
